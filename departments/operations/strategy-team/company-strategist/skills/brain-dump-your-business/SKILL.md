# Brain-Dump Your Business

Interview the owner about their business, out loud, one question at a time, then turn what they said into the shared brain. This is the single most important conversation in the whole program: every team member they ever set up reads what gets written today.

## Say this first
Before the first question, tell the owner what this is in plain words, something like: "I'm going to ask you nine questions about your business, one at a time, and you just talk. Messy is good. Everything you say gets written into one file that every team member you ever set up reads first, so they know your business before they do anything. It takes about 30 minutes. If you get stuck on one, we skip it and come back later."

Keep it moving. If they go blank on a question (not if they're rambling, which is good), offer a guess where allowed (see below). If that doesn't land either, file it under Still to Answer and go to the next question.

## How to run the interview
- ONE question at a time. Ask, then wait. Never dump the list.
- They answer out loud with Wispr. If an answer opens an interesting door, follow it before moving on: the detours are where the good material lives.
- Messy is good. Never rush them to be concise. Long rambling answers beat tidy short ones.
- No business yet? Have them answer for the one they're building, and mark everything written today as a starting guess to sharpen when the first real customer shows up.

## Read the repo and guess as you go
Before the first question, read `business.md`, everything in `brand/`, and `knowledge/business-audit.md`. On a fresh department most of it is empty, and that's expected. Your guesses come from two places: anything already written, and the owner's own earlier answers. If the owner shares a website link or pastes their about page, use that too. If you can't open the link, say so and ask them to paste the text.

From the second question on, offer 2 or 3 candidate answers built from what they've already said, labeled as guesses, like: "You said you do roofs for homeowners, so my guess for who else they could pick: the two big roofing companies in town, a handyman, or doing nothing until it leaks. Who do you actually lose jobs to?" People react to a guess faster than they answer a blank question, and their reaction is still their answer, so file only what they confirm or fix, never the guess itself. Never guess on questions 2, 3 or 9. Those answers must be the owner's own numbers, stories and words. Guess only on 1, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8.

## The questions, in order
1. What do you sell, and who actually buys it?
2. What does a customer pay you the first time? Do they come back, and how many times? And how many more customers could you actually take on this month before you'd be underwater?
3. Walk me through your last three customers, one at a time: how did each one actually first hear of you?
4. What does your week actually look like? Where do the hours really go?
5. What do you hate doing?
6. What marketing have you already tried that didn't work, and what would you flat-out refuse to do, no matter how well it works for other people?
7. What's already written down somewhere, and where does it live?
8. Who else could your customers go to instead of you? Name names.
9. What do most people in your industry get completely wrong?

## Help them answer: what a good answer has
Read the matching line if an answer comes back thin, so they know what you're after:
1. **What you sell:** the thing, and the actual person who pays. "Roofs" is thin. "New roofs and repairs for homeowners in two counties, mostly people in their 50s" is enough.
2. **The money:** three numbers, rough is fine. First sale, how many times they come back, and how many more customers they could take this month. If they're stuck, do the math out loud: first sale times times-back is roughly what a customer is worth.
3. **The last three customers:** one at a time, and for each one the exact way they found the business. "Word of mouth" is thin; "my neighbor's brother saw my truck" is real.
4. **The week:** where the hours go, with rough hours. Try four buckets: finding customers, closing them, doing the work, and running the business.
5. **What you hate doing:** the job they'd hand off tomorrow if they could.
6. **Tried and won't do:** one thing that flopped, and one thing they'd never do, like dancing on video or cold calling.
7. **Already written down:** templates, photos, reviews, an old website, a deck, and where each one lives.
8. **Who else they could pick:** real names. If they say "nobody", ask what a customer does instead of paying them, because doing nothing counts.
9. **What your industry gets wrong:** if they're stuck, try the two-column trick. Have them name three things everybody in their industry does, then say what they do differently or think is wrong about each one. Keep their exact words, because the Contrarian Take lesson builds from this quote.

## Three worked examples
Use these only when an answer comes back thin. Share just the matching part of the closest example (one sentence), never the whole paragraph, and never before they've tried to answer. Short versions:
- **Local service (Marcus, a two-truck roofing company):** sells new roofs and repairs to homeowners in two counties. About 9,000 a roof, they come back once in 20 years but send neighbors, and he could take three more jobs a month. Last three customers: a neighbor saw his truck, a Google search, and a past customer's referral. Hates chasing payment. Tried Angi, it flopped; won't do door-knocking. Already has 300 before and after photos and 40 Google reviews. Loses jobs to the two big companies in town. Industry gets wrong: "Everybody upsells a full roof when half the time it's a 600 dollar repair."
- **Coach or consultant (Priya, who coaches therapists opening a private practice):** sells a 3-month coaching program to therapists leaving agency jobs. About 3,000 per client, some renew once, and she could take four more. Last three: a podcast, a LinkedIn post, and a referral from a supervisor. Hates writing LinkedIn posts. Tried Facebook ads, lost money; won't do "6-figure practice" hype. Already has an intake workbook and 30 recorded calls. Clients could pick a practice-building course, a business coach for everyone, or a friend who did it. Industry gets wrong: "They teach marketing before the therapist knows who they want to help."
- **Product (Hannah, who sells hand-poured candles online):** sells candles to women who buy them as gifts and for themselves. About 32 an order, good buyers come back three or four times a year, and she could pour twice what she sells now. Last three: Instagram, Etsy search, and a friend's gift. Hates answering "where's my order" emails. Tried a local market booth, too slow; won't do discount codes on every post. Already has 22 scent descriptions and a 2,000-person email list. Shoppers could pick Bath & Body Works or any Etsy maker. Industry gets wrong: "Everyone sells the jar. Nobody tells you how to burn it so it lasts."

These are made-up examples to show the level of detail. Never write them into business.md.

## Fill in business.md
`business.md` already has every heading waiting, each with a `_Not filled in yet._` line. Write their answers under the matching heading, replacing that line, and keep the headings, the top note and the `## Updates` section. Fill the `## At a Glance` block too. Leave `What You Run On` for the walk-your-business skill, which fills it next. The headings, mapped to the questions above:
- **What You Sell** (Q1): what they sell, and who actually buys it.
- **The Economics** (Q2): first-sale price, whether customers come back and how many times, and monthly capacity before they're underwater.
- **How Customers Find You** (Q3): how the last three customers actually heard of them, in their own words, one at a time.
- **The Week** (Q4): where the hours really go.
- **What You Hate Doing** (Q5): the work they'd hand off first.
- **Won't Do / Already Tried** (Q6): marketing that failed, and what they'd flat-out refuse to do. Every planning skill reads this section before proposing a channel; a plan the owner silently abandons is worse than no plan.
- **What's Already Written Down** (Q7): what exists and where it lives.
- **Who Else They Could Pick** (Q8): the competitor names, plain. The research team's target list starts here.
- **Still to Answer**: thin or skipped answers, filed here, not asked in the moment (see below).
Their own words, cleaned but never corporate. Then add one dated line to `## Updates`: what got filled in today.

Question 9 doesn't go in business.md. It gets saved word for word into `brand/contrarian-take.md` under "## Raw Material From the Brain Dump": the find-your-contrarian-take skill builds the contrarian take from this exact quote, so keep their exact phrasing.

Anything about clients or customer details follows the same privacy rules as everywhere else: facts about their business yes, other people's private information no.

## If a question gets skipped or the answer is thin
Don't push in the moment, note it. At the end of the interview, add to business.md's "Still to Answer" heading:

    - (date) What's a customer worth? Skipped, come back to this.

Then, in ANY later session that touches the business file, ask ONE open question from that list, just one, woven in naturally, and file the answer where it belongs, removing it from the list. The list shrinking to nothing is part of the program working. Never ambush them with the whole list.

## The ending, always
1. Read back the three most important things you heard, in one line each. Let them correct you.
2. Fill in the `## At a Glance` block at the top of `business.md` from what they said (business name first). Then run the sync command (`python3 tools/sync.py`, or `py tools/sync.py` on Windows), open `command-center/index.html`, and tell them: that's your company now, teams waiting to be set up. Have them take a screenshot for the before-and-after later.
3. Save the work to GitHub: run the github agent's save-work skill at `departments/it-systems/dev-team/github-agent/skills/save-work/`. Their business is now somewhere safer than one laptop.

## Done when
`business.md` reads like a real business talked in by a real person, no `_Not filled in yet._` line is left in any section it fills, the contrarian raw material is filed, the Still to Answer heading exists (even if empty), the Command Center shows their company, and the work is saved.
